# Declaration Draft

Turn what the client actually told the firm into a clear, credible, first-person declaration — as a **draft for attorney and client review**, not a final filing and not a document the client has yet adopted.

## The non-negotiable rule

**Draft only from facts the client or attorney has provided. Never invent, embellish, assume, or "fill in" facts, dates, places, names, or events.** A declaration is sworn testimony that anchors the client's credibility under the REAL ID Act; a single fabricated or guessed detail can sink the entire case and expose the client and the attorney. Where the narrative has a gap, a missing date, or an ambiguity, **insert a bracketed flag** — e.g., `[CLARIFY WITH CLIENT: approximate date of the second detention?]` — rather than guessing. Gaps flagged for the attorney are the correct output; invented facts are never acceptable.

## Before anything

Read `PRACTICE_PROFILE.md` for **house style** (declaration tone and format, first-person conventions, signature/interpreter blocks) and the **work-product header**. Note which relief is in play (from `claim-evaluation` if it's been run) so the narrative deliberately covers the elements that relief requires.

## What the declaration must cover

Organize the client's own account so it naturally establishes each element — without argument (save argument for the brief):

- **Identity and background** — who the client is, family, community, and the characteristics tied to the protected ground (ethnicity, religion, political activity, group membership, etc.).
- **Chronology of harm** — what happened, in order, in concrete sensory detail the client actually recalls: dates or approximate dates, places, who did what, what was said, injuries, threats. Specificity is what reads as credible — but only specificity the client supplied.
- **Nexus in the client's own words** — why the client believes they were targeted (the persecutor's statements, insignia, pattern), so the "on account of" link emerges from the facts.
- **Fear of return** — what the client believes will happen if returned, and why.
- **Why the government won't protect them / why relocation isn't safe** — the client's own experience of reporting to authorities (or why they couldn't) and why moving elsewhere in the country wouldn't help.
- **Timeliness facts** *(asylum)* — if the one-year deadline is an issue, the facts bearing on a changed- or extraordinary-circumstances exception (again, only real facts).

## Consistency check

Before finishing, cross-check the draft against any I-589, prior statement, or intake already in the record. Surface every inconsistency to the attorney in a short note at the end — dates that don't line up, events in a different order, names spelled differently. Do not silently "correct" them; the attorney resolves them with the client.

## Output format

Under the work-product header, produce:

1. **The declaration** in house style: numbered paragraphs, first person, past tense for events; identity/background → chronology → nexus → fear → protection/relocation → timeliness (if relevant).
2. **Signature block** and, where the client's language requires it, an **interpreter/translation certification** block (the declarant read it in a language they understand, or it was read back through a competent interpreter).
3. **Flags for the attorney** — a bulleted list of every `[CLARIFY]` item, every consistency issue, and every place corroboration should be lined up to match the narrative.

## Guardrails

- Draft, not filing. The client must review, correct, and adopt it; the attorney supervises.
- No invented or embellished facts — ever. Flag gaps; never fill them.
- Do not coach the client toward a "better" story or suggest facts that would strengthen an element. Articulate what's there; identify what's missing.
- Keep the client's voice; avoid legal conclusions and argument in the declaration itself.
- Respect heightened confidentiality and the client's safety — this document names people and places that can endanger the client and others.

# House format for asylum interview question sets

Reverse-engineered from the firm's generic template and one fully client-adapted build of the same instrument. Confidence is marked so you can tell an observed convention from a recommendation.

- **HIGH** - present in both source documents in the same form. Treat as a firm rule.
- **MEDIUM** - present in one in a way that generalizes.
- **ADDED** - not observed in the firm's documents. Recommended on asylum-practice grounds. Every ADDED item is labeled wherever it appears so the attorney can strike it.

---

## 1. Fixed architecture

**Part 1 is the verbatim security block. (HIGH)** See `security-block.md`.

**Part 2 is Travel History. (HIGH)** The firm's template titles this correctly but fills it only with trips abroad. It must also establish entry into the United States, or the instrument leaves the officer's opening questions uncovered.

**Part 3 is Asylum Application Review. (HIGH)** Verification of the I-589 and the supporting documents.

**The final part is Closing Questions. (HIGH)** Three functions: anything to add to the claim, any other feared harm not yet described, and a comprehension check asking whether the client misunderstood any question. The comprehension check preserves credibility if a translation problem surfaces later. It is never dropped.

**The middle is modular, ordered by claim logic. (HIGH)** Between Application Review and Closing Questions the sequence runs: identity and group membership, then what happened, then who did it, then why the state will not help, then why the client cannot relocate, then what is feared on return.

The firm's template violates its own logic here by placing the protected-ground section after the persecutor sections. The adapted version corrects this. Establish the protected ground before examining the harm, because the harm only means anything once the ground is on the table.

Some parts sit outside that sequence entirely: family composition, nationality and statelessness, per-theory parts, life in the United States, and the spouse and derivative parts. Their placement is a judgment call, not an ordering rule.

## 2. Question craft

**One fact per question. (HIGH)** No compound questions outside the security block.

**Escalation ladders. (HIGH)** Open broad and narrow in sequence: screening question, then count, then timing, then actor, then open narrative. The firm's pattern runs: have you ever been threatened, how many times, when did these occur, who threatened you, can you describe what happened. Jumping straight to narrative loses the frame the narrative needs.

**Exhaustion sweeps. (HIGH)** Close every enumerated list of actors or motives with a sweep: is there any other reason, is there any other way, did they say anything else.

An officer will often not ask these. They are in the document for the client's protection, not because they predict the interview. A basis the client leaves unstated at the interview and raises later is exactly how adverse credibility findings get made. Do not let anyone strip these as padding.

**Open-ended, never leading. (HIGH, one functional exception)** Three failure modes, and the third is the one that slips through: the question embeds its answer; the question invites confirmation; or the question presupposes the conclusion it is supposed to elicit. "Why couldn't you relocate to another area?" presupposes that the client could not, which is the very thing the client must establish in their own words. Ask whether there is anywhere they could live safely, then why not.

The exception is Application Review, where the function is verifying a document and confirmation-form questions are correct by design.

**Plain language. (HIGH)** No legal terminology anywhere in the client-facing text. Nexus, particular social group, well-founded fear, and persecutor all get converted. "Why would they target you specifically" is a nexus question written for a human being.

**Second person, with named handoffs. (HIGH)** The applicant is "you" throughout. When the instrument turns to another family member it names them and addresses them directly, courtesy title for the spouse and first name for adult children, then continues in second person.

**Identity and belief are tested, not accepted. (HIGH)** Do not accept a claimed affiliation and move on. Ask what the belief system holds, why a specific practice matters, what a specific office or title means, and the client's own role and frequency of participation. The officer will probe this, and a client who cannot explain their own faith or identity in their own words has a credibility problem regardless of how true the claim is. Extend this to every derivative who is old enough to answer.

**Definitional questions for culturally loaded terms. (MEDIUM)** Where the claim turns on a concept the officer may not share, ask the client to define it themselves rather than assuming shared meaning. This builds the record and tests whether the client understands the framework they are invoking.

## 3. Adapting the template to a client

This is the part of the house standard that is directly observable, because the firm's two documents are the same instrument before and after adaptation.

**Anchor questions to the record, and name the source. (HIGH)** Prefix questions that test a specific assertion: "You stated in your declaration that ...", "You mentioned ...", "Your wife mentioned an incident where ...". This tells the client which record statement is being tested, demonstrates the drafter did not invent the fact, and surfaces cross-declarant material. That last one matters most: where a fact appears only in the spouse's statement, the principal applicant will still be asked about it and must be ready.

**Every discrete incident gets its own labeled block. (HIGH)** The template uses placeholders (First Threat, Second Threat). The adapted version replaces them with the actual incident names, each introduced by a short label ending in a colon, then drills down: when, where, who was present, who else was involved, what exactly was said or done, what happened immediately after, whether it was reported, and what the authorities did. Never fold two incidents into one block.

**Enumerate every feared actor separately, and number them. (HIGH)** Each gets its own why-this-client drill-down, whether that actor ever threatened the client directly, whether that actor has harmed anyone else for the same reason, and its own exhaustion sweep.

**One dedicated part per distinct persecution theory. (HIGH)** Blending theories loses the elements that only one of them supplies.

**Government protection, whenever any persecutor is not the state. (HIGH)** Reporting, frequency, police response, investigation, arrests, why the client believes protection would be withheld, whether any law protects the client's group, and whether such laws are actually enforced. That last question is the one that does the work. A protective statute on the books is the government's best answer, and enforcement in practice is the client's.

**Life in the United States. (MEDIUM)** Arrival, residence, work, schooling, and continued practice of the protected characteristic here. Builds continuity of identity.

**Every derivative questioned separately, by name. (HIGH)** Their own identity, their own experience, their own fear, and their fear for the principal applicant. Derivatives do not ride along on the principal's answers.

**Family composition. (MEDIUM)** Who is on the application, who is not, and where parents, siblings, and other relatives currently live. Where relatives are themselves a feared source of harm, their location is substantive, not background.

**Nationality and statelessness, where applicable. (MEDIUM)** Ask the client to explain their statelessness in their own words, what travel documents they hold, whether they ever held or applied for citizenship anywhere, and whether there is any country where they have a right to citizenship they have not pursued.

That last question is potentially dispositive, but label what it goes to correctly. An unexercised right to citizenship is **not** firm resettlement, which requires an offer of permanent status received before arrival in the United States. It goes to the refugee definition itself, where an applicant holding more than one nationality must establish fear as to each country of nationality. Verify the governing authority before relying on this in argument.

## 4. Element coverage the instrument must reach

| Element or issue | How the house format reaches it |
|---|---|
| Statutory bars | Part 1 security block |
| Firm resettlement | Status or offer of permanent status received in a third country before arrival; protection sought in transit countries and why not |
| Additional nationality | Unpursued right to citizenship elsewhere |
| Date and manner of entry | Travel History, including port of entry and visa type |
| One-year filing deadline | Measured from LAST ARRIVAL, not first entry [ADDED] |
| Return trips to the country of feared harm | Travel History |
| Application accuracy and credibility | Part 3, including corrections, who assisted, document genuineness, documents not submitted |
| Protected ground | Identity or belief background part, with substantive knowledge questions |
| Past persecution | Incident-by-incident drill-downs |
| Nexus | Why would this actor target you specifically |
| State unwilling or unable to protect | Government Protection part, law versus enforcement |
| Internal relocation | Named cities, plus any actual relocation attempt |
| How persecutors would learn of return | Dedicated part |
| Ongoing search for the client | Recency of last contact |
| Well-founded fear | Severity, likelihood, timing |
| Derivative eligibility | Separate parts per derivative |

## 5. Additions to the house standard

Eight items below are **not** in the firm's existing templates. Each is labeled [ADDED] wherever it appears so the attorney can strike it. Only the first is treated as blocking in the review skill.

1. **One-year filing deadline.** The single largest gap in the firm's current templates. Neither asks when the client filed or why any delay is excused. Measured from the client's **last arrival** in the United States, not first entry, which matters precisely because this instrument also collects return trips. Verify the current rule.
2. **Interview orientation.** An unnumbered "Before the Interview" section preceding Part 1: the oath; who supplies the interpreter and who may serve as one; the client's right to say when an interpretation is wrong; presence of counsel; biometrics; and that "I do not know" and "I do not remember" are truthful answers where true and better than a guess. As of the last verification the applicant must bring their own competent interpreter, who must be an adult fluent in both languages and may not be the attorney or a witness, and appearing without one can be treated as a failure to appear. Confirm the current rule before every interview, since it has changed more than once.
3. **Prior statements the officer will hold the client to.** The visa application and embassy interview, statements at the port of entry or to CBP, any credible or reasonable fear interview, and any prior immigration filing. This is where adverse credibility findings actually originate, and the firm's templates do not touch it.
4. **US-side history.** Overstay or unlawful presence, arrests or charges in the United States, prior removal orders.
5. **Torture and government acquiescence, for CAT.** Asked separately from general fear. Note that an affirmative asylum officer ordinarily adjudicates asylum only and refers withholding and CAT to the immigration court, so this is preparation for the record and a later hearing rather than a prediction of the affirmative interview. Ask it anyway.
6. **Corroboration.** What evidence the client could reasonably have obtained and did not, and why not.
7. **Mixed motive and imputed grounds.** Whether the persecutor acted for more than one reason, and whether the persecutor believed the client held a belief, opinion, or identity the client does not actually hold.
8. **Particular social group questions,** where any theory rests on a PSG: social recognition, whether people in that society would know who belongs, and immutability.

## 6. Formatting

- Title naming the instrument and claim type; the adapted version appends the client name.
- Each part as a second-level heading, formatted `Part N: Title`.
- Questions as a flat list, one per line, no numbering inside a part.
- Sub-groupings introduced by a short label ending in a colon: an incident name or a person's name.
- The question set body carries questions only. No answer notes, no commentary, no citations. The two permitted exceptions are bracketed record-gap flags and, in a reusable template, bracketed placeholders with drafting instructions.
